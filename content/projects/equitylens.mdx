<Section n="01" id="overview" title="Overview" kicker="Problem · Context · What I built">

## Problem

Healthcare AI makes decisions about people — who gets prioritised in triage,
which treatment is recommended, who needs follow-up. And almost nobody is
checking whether those decisions are equally accurate across the populations
they affect.

A model trained predominantly on one demographic performs measurably worse for
others. That is not a bug in the model; it is how learning from skewed data
works. In healthcare it has clinical consequences.

## Context

I started this because I was angry about a gap, not because it was a good
product idea. The gap: the EU AI Act requires "appropriate transparency" for
systems affecting fundamental rights, but a compliance audit is expensive and
slow, so in practice small hospitals and clinics do not perform one at all.

Meanwhile the people who could do the audit are not the people who deploy the
models. The tooling gap is the real barrier.

## What I built

EquityLens is a fairness auditing platform. It evaluates a model across
demographic axes and reports:

- **A fairness metrics engine** — demographic parity, equalized odds and
  calibration, computed across each sensitive attribute and compared
  pairwise.
- **Causal attribution modules** — distinguishing genuine model bias from
  confounding variables in the data, because detecting a gap is only half the
  job.
- **Interactive Pareto frontiers** — the actual trade-off curve between
  accuracy and each fairness metric, so a stakeholder can see precisely what
  they would be giving up to improve a number.
- **Automated drift detection** — fairness metrics tracked over time, because
  bias creeps in as data distributions move and a one-off audit misses it.
- **Compliance mapping** — findings expressed against EU AI Act risk
  categories, NIST AI RMF governance functions, and ISO/IEC 25059.

</Section>

<Section n="02" id="system" title="System" kicker="Architecture · Data flow · Infrastructure">

## Architecture

A FastAPI service that takes a model or prediction set plus a sensitive
attribute, and produces a fairness report.

<FairnessAuditDiagram caption="The audit path. Metrics are computed first, attributed second, and only then mapped to a regulatory obligation — mapping before attribution produces confident nonsense." />

## Data flow

Predictions + ground truth + sensitive attribute → six fairness metrics per
attribute group → causal attribution tests to separate model bias from data
bias → Pareto frontier across accuracy and each metric → regulatory mapping →
drift monitoring over successive evaluation windows.

The ordering is the design. Computing attribution before mapping matters:
a regulatory finding that says *this model is non-compliant* is useless if the
underlying gap turns out to be a sampling artefact.

## Infrastructure

Python service with a React front end, PostgreSQL for audit-run history so
drift can be computed across runs rather than within one, and TensorFlow for
the model-side evaluation.

</Section>

<Section n="03" id="engineering" title="Engineering" kicker="Decisions · Tradeoffs · Failures">

## Important decisions

**Attribution before mapping.** The temptation is to go from metric to
compliance finding directly, because that is the artefact anybody wants. It is
also how you ship a report that says *your model is unlawful* when the real
cause is a hospital’s sampling of a particular ward. Attribution first means
the compliance layer describes a cause, not just a number.

**Show the trade-off, do not optimise it.** Fairness and accuracy genuinely
conflict. Any single "fairness score" hides a Pareto frontier. The interface
shows the curve and lets the stakeholder choose a point on it, because that
choice is a governance decision, not an engineering one.

**Causal inference is the hard part.** "This group gets a different outcome" is
cheap. "This model causes that difference" requires controlling for
confounders that are genuinely not identifiable from observational data in
every case. The platform is explicit about which claims it can and cannot
support, because the alternative is a confident and wrong causal story.

## Tradeoffs

- **Diagnostic depth vs. time-to-answer.** A full causal analysis is slow. The
  platform offers a fast metric-only pass and a deep attributed pass, and is
  clear about which one produced a given result.
- **Broad metric coverage vs. interpretation risk.** Six metrics is more than
  most audits compute, and each additional metric invites a stakeholder to
  optimise the one that is easiest. The interface makes the conflict explicit
  rather than presenting them as independently improvable.
- **Research rigor vs. deployability.** Some of the causally-correct methods
  need assumptions the healthcare data cannot satisfy. Being honest about the
  assumptions is worth more than reporting a clean number.

## Failures

The first version mapped directly from raw metrics to compliance language. It
produced confident, specific, and occasionally entirely wrong regulatory
findings, because the metric was real but the cause was not ours to attribute.
Adding the attribution stage fixed the class of problem rather than the
symptom.

</Section>

<Section n="04" id="result" title="Result" kicker="What worked · What changed · What I learned">

## What worked

Running it against a public, de-identified healthcare triage dataset surfaced
a **23% performance gap** across demographic groups. That number is the reason
the project exists, and it was not a subtle finding — it was large enough that
a team using such a model in production would almost certainly have noticed
bad outcomes without understanding why.

Reducing a manual fairness review from days to under an hour, with a
reproducible artefact, is the other result that matters operationally.

## What changed

The framing of fairness itself. I went in thinking it was a property to be
maximised. It is a Pareto trade-off to be chosen deliberately and documented,
by people who own the consequences.

## What I learned

- Fairness is not a checkbox. It requires continuous monitoring because bias
  arrives through distribution drift, long after the audit.
- Detecting a disparity and explaining it are different problems with
  different difficulty, and only the first is easy.
- Compliance mappings to EU AI Act and NIST RMF are what turn an academic
  metric into an actionable finding.
- The accuracy-fairness trade-off is real. Any product that presents these as
  independently improvable is selling something.

</Section>
