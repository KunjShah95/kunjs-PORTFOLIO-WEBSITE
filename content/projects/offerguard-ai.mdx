<Section n="01" id="overview" title="Overview" kicker="Problem · Context · What I built">

## Problem

Job offers are documents designed to look good. The recruiter is on your side
right up until they are not. Ghost roles, bait-and-switch titles and burnout
culture hide behind phrases like *fast-paced* and *wear many hats* — and the
existing tools either give you anonymous glassdoor sentiment or cost you a
lawyer. Nobody was reading the actual text of your offer and telling you what
to look at.

## Context

The request came from a friend who had an offer that looked strong on paper and
felt wrong in a way they could not articulate. *Can you build something that
reads this and tells me what to look out for?* I said yes before I knew how.

The consequence of getting this wrong is asymmetric. A missed red flag costs
someone some caution. A false positive costs someone a job they wanted — and
that asymmetry dominated every subsequent design decision.

## What I built

OfferGuard accepts three inputs — a job description, an offer letter, and a
recorder chat transcript — and analyses them together across five dimensions:

- **Toxicity signals** — language patterns correlated with poor environments
- **Burnout risk** — unreasonable expectations framed as opportunity
- **Salary fairness** — market comparison on role, location and experience
- **Ghost job detection** — signals the role may not be real
- **Negotiation strategy** — specific talking points to actually use

No signup. Nothing stored. Paste, read, leave.

</Section>

<Section n="02" id="system" title="System" kicker="Architecture · Data flow · Infrastructure">

## Architecture

A three-stage model chain rather than one prompt.

The **primary scanner** triages the whole input cheaply and decides which
regions are worth a second look. The **deep analyser** then extracts structured
signals from just those regions. The **cross-check model** re-reads the result
and looks for internal inconsistency — claims that contradict the text they
claim to summarise.

<RouterDiagram caption="Three stages with different jobs. Triage narrows, extraction structures, cross-check audits. Running all three over the full document is three times the cost for a worse answer." />

## Data flow

Input → cheap triage pass that marks regions of interest → targeted deep
extraction on those regions only → cross-check pass for consistency → structured
result rendered with the flagged sentences highlighted in the original text.

Highlighting sentences in context rather than returning a score was a deliberate
choice. The score is the least useful output; *this phrase, here, because* is
what a person can act on.

## Infrastructure

TanStack Start with React 19 on Vercel. Firebase for the lightweight
server-side pieces. Groq for inference — the latency is the product here, and
this is a use case where a two-second answer is meaningfully different from a
twelve-second one.

</Section>

<Section n="03" id="engineering" title="Engineering" kicker="Decisions · Tradeoffs · Failures">

## Important decisions

**Cross-model consistency is the hard problem.** Different models disagree on
subjective signals like toxicity, and a system that silently averages them
produces a number with no meaning. The cross-check stage exists specifically to
surface disagreement rather than hide it.

**Highlight, do not score.** A confidence percentage invites people to treat
the output as a verdict. Highlighting the sentence with its explanation invites
them to read it. The second is a request; the first is an assertion you cannot
support.

**Say when you might be wrong.** There is a human disclaimer in the interface,
not a legal one: *I am a tool, not a judge. Use me as input, not truth.* I
added it after the incident described in
[When AI Gets It Wrong](/writing/when-ai-gets-it-wrong), and it should have been
there from the start.

## Tradeoffs

- **Triage precision vs. recall.** Triage that is too aggressive skips the
  sentence that mattered. It errs toward reading more than strictly necessary —
  the cost of a wasted deep pass is far lower than the cost of a missed flag.
- **Speed vs. depth.** A single deep pass over everything is simpler and slower
  in aggregate. Triage-then-target is more machinery and a much better p95.
- **Actionability vs. objectivity.** "This looks toxic" is objective and
  useless. "This phrase, in this context, may indicate X" is less clean and
  vastly more useful.

## Failures

The system flagged an offer as toxic that was not. The flagged phrases —
*fast-paced*, *wear many hats* — genuinely are risk indicators. What the system
lacked was the context that this particular company was a normal, enthusiastic
startup. It saw patterns, not people.

The response was not to make the model more conservative. It was to change what
the system claims. It now returns signals and reasoning, and every output is
framed as something a person can disagree with.

</Section>

<Section n="04" id="result" title="Result" kicker="What worked · What changed · What I learned">

## What worked

The sentence highlighting. People do not want a verdict, they want to know
which words to look at — and once you show them the words, most of them
disagree with the system and that is fine, because the system gave them
something they can reason about instead of something to obey.

The multi-provider chain proved its worth immediately in consistency terms.

## What changed

The framing, completely. This began as a scoring product and became an
information product. Accuracy did not change; what the system was willing to
assert did.

## What I learned

- Accuracy is not sufficient. A system can be 95% accurate and still hurt
  someone in the 5%, and if it is affecting a real decision the edge cases are
  people, not bugs.
- A flag is useful. A verdict is dangerous.
- Build room for a human to say *I disagree*, or you have built a machine that
  launders its own uncertainty into false confidence.

</Section>
