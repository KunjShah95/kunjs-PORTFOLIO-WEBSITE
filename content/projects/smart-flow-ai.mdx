<Section n="01" id="overview" title="Overview" kicker="Problem · Context · What I built">

## Problem

Crowd management in transit hubs is reactive. Staff find out about a
bottleneck when it is already a bottleneck, and then respond to the situation
rather than to the trend that caused it. Peak-hour congestion is the visible
symptom of a forecasting problem.

## Context

The operations team is the user, not a passenger. That distinction drives
everything. A passenger wants an accurate number. An operator wants to know
where to put two extra staff in the next twenty minutes, and a dashboard that
requires interpretation to produce that decision is a dashboard that does not
get used.

## What I built

A real-time monitoring platform for transit hubs:

- **Live density heatmaps** layered onto hub maps, fed by continuous sensor
  ingestion, so the current state is visible without a page refresh.
- **Wait-time prediction** across a 24-hour forecast window.
- **Operational alerting** on predicted rather than observed conditions — the
  only version of this product that is worth building.

</Section>

<Section n="02" id="system" title="System" kicker="Architecture · Data flow · Infrastructure">

## Architecture

<RouterDiagram caption="Hybrid routing. A statistical estimator handles the quiet hours; the ML model takes over when there is enough signal to justify it." />

## Data flow

Sensor stream → Firestore realtime listener → normalisation and resampling to
a fixed cadence → model selection by signal density → prediction with a
confidence band → alert evaluation against operator-configured thresholds →
map overlay and dashboard.

## Infrastructure

Firebase Firestore as the realtime ingestion layer because the workload is
write-heavy, append-only, and read by a small number of live dashboards.
Sub-500ms from sensor to view.

</Section>

<Section n="03" id="engineering" title="Engineering" kicker="Decisions · Tradeoffs · Failures">

## Important decisions

**Hybrid statistical + ML, not pure ML.** A pure ML model on this data
performs worse than a well-specified statistical estimator during the quiet
hours, because there is not enough signal to generalise from and the model
interpolates confidently between two sparse points. The hybrid routes to
statistics when signal density is low and to the model when it is not. It is
less elegant and it is right.

**Predict, do not just display.** The first version was a dashboard. It was
correct and it was ignored, because a dashboard requires the operator to
interpret a picture in order to make a decision. Alerting on *predicted*
conditions is what turned it into a tool.

**Resample to a fixed cadence.** Sensor data arrives irregularly. Feeding it
to a model at its native rate makes the model's input distribution depend on
the network, which is not something you want in a forecasting system.

## Tradeoffs

- **Hybrid complexity vs. model purity.** Two paths mean two things to get
  wrong and a routing boundary to get wrong too. Pure ML would have been
  simpler to explain and worse in production.
- **Alerting vs. fatigue.** Alerting on every predicted threshold crossing
  trains operators to ignore alerts. The thresholds are operator-configurable
  precisely because the right value is site-specific and unknowable in advance.
- **24-hour window vs. accuracy.** Long horizons are where pure forecasting
  degrades fastest. The window is capped at what the data can support.

## Failures

Off-peak hours were the persistent failure. The model produced confident
predictions from almost no data, and the operational consequence was false
alarms at exactly the times when staff were least inclined to trust the system.
The statistical fallback for low signal density resolved it.

</Section>

<Section n="04" id="result" title="Result" kicker="What worked · What changed · What I learned">

## What worked

94% wait-time prediction accuracy with a 24-hour window, at under 500ms from
sensor to view, with alerting on predicted rather than observed state.

## What changed

The definition of the product. It started as a visualisation problem and is an
operations problem. The visualisation is the output; the decision it supports
is the product.

## What I learned

- Hybrid statistical and ML models beat pure ML on noisy real-time data, and
  the hybrid is not a compromise when the data has regime changes.
- Operations teams need actionable alerts. Dashboards that require
  interpretation do not get acted on.
- Confidence has to be part of the prediction, not a separate concern. A system
  that is confidently wrong during quiet hours loses the operator's trust
  permanently.

</Section>
