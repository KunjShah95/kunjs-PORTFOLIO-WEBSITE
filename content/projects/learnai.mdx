<Section n="01" id="overview" title="Overview" kicker="Problem · Context · What I built">

## Problem

E-learning platforms deliver the same content to everyone. A student who
already understands it is bored; a student who does not is lost, and neither
gets a different experience. Completion rates fall, and the content quality is
not the thing that failed.

## Context

The intervention is not "AI-generated lessons" — that was not the bottleneck.
The bottleneck is that difficulty is fixed at authoring time. Content is written
for an imagined median student, so it is simultaneously too easy for the top of
the distribution and too hard for the bottom.

## What I built

LearnAI estimates a student's proficiency per concept and re-tunes the next
question's difficulty against it, live.

- **Proficiency estimation** — a per-concept mastery signal derived from the
  student's response history, not a single aggregate score. Aggregation hides
  exactly the information an adaptive system needs.
- **Adaptive difficulty** — five calibrated levels, selected per question from
  the proficiency signal.
- **Spaced repetition scheduling** — review intervals computed per student from
  their own performance history rather than from a global schedule.
- **Multi-provider inference** — three providers behind one interface, with
  fallback between them.

</Section>

## Architecture

## Data flow

Response → per-concept proficiency update → difficulty selection for the next
item → question generation or retrieval at that level → spaced-repetition
schedule recomputed → Firebase realtime persistence of the session so a
refresh does not lose progress.

## Infrastructure

Next.js with Supabase for the relational student model and Firebase for
realtime session state. The split is deliberate: durable relational data and
ephemeral session state have genuinely different shapes and different
consistency requirements, and forcing both into one store would have been
worse at both.

</Section>

<Section n="03" id="engineering" title="Engineering" kicker="Decisions · Tradeoffs · Failures">

## Important decisions

**Proficiency per concept, not per student.** A single mastery number is
average of a distribution, and averaging a distribution destroys the one
signal an adaptive system exists to use. Per-concept tracking is more work and
it is the entire mechanism.

**Fallback is a correctness requirement, not a feature.** A lesson that stops
because a provider had a bad minute is a broken lesson. The 99.7% uptime figure
comes from treating provider fallback as part of the critical path rather than
as an enhancement.

**Difficulty is calibrated, not arbitrary.** Five levels sounds like a
simplification, and it is — but an uncalibrated continuous scale is harder to
tune and harder to reason about than five well-chosen bands.

## Tradeoffs

- **Granularity vs. cold start.** Per-concept tracking is better but is empty on
  day one. The system handles sparse history by falling back to a cohort prior
  until a student's own signal accumulates.
- **Challenge vs. discouragement.** Pushing difficulty too aggressively loses
  students permanently. The pacing curve is deliberately not maximal — this is
  the single most sensitive constant in the product.
- **Provider flexibility vs. output consistency.** Three providers means three
  slightly different output shapes. A normalisation layer absorbs it at the cost
  of one more thing that can be wrong.

## Failures

The first difficulty curve was too aggressive and produced a steep drop in
completion partway through a session: students were being promoted past material
they had not consolidated, hitting questions they could not answer, and leaving.
Flattening the early curve recovered most of the completion rate. The 45% lift
is against the tuned system, not the first one.

</Section>

<Section n="04" id="result" title="Result" kicker="What worked · What changed · What I learned">

## What worked

A 45% lift in quiz completion against the fixed-difficulty version, with
student satisfaction at 4.2/5. The lift came almost entirely from pacing rather
than from content, which was not the hypothesis when the project started.

## What changed

The assumption that content was the variable. The content was adequate. The
scheduling was the defect.

## What I learned

- Adaptive pacing matters more than content quality for engagement.
- Averaging a mastery signal across concepts destroys the information value.
- Multi-provider fallback is not resilience theatre; it is a correctness
  requirement for anything a learner is depending on in real time.
- Under-promise difficulty. A student who is challenged and finishes is worth
  far more than one who is challenged and quits.

</Section>
