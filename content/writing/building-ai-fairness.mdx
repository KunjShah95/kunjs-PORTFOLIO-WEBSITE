I started this project because I was angry.

Healthcare algorithms were making decisions about patient care — who gets
prioritised, what treatment is recommended, which patients need follow-up. And
nobody was checking whether these systems were fair.

## The problem

A model trained mostly on data from one demographic performs worse for others.
This is not a bug. It is how learning from skewed data works. Models learn
patterns from their training data; skewed data, skewed model.

The problem is that in healthcare, skewed decisions have real consequences. A
triage system that underestimates severity can delay critical care. A treatment
recommender that works better for one group creates unequal outcomes.

The EU AI Act now requires transparency for systems affecting fundamental
rights. But compliance is expensive, and most small hospitals and clinics do
not have the resources to audit their models.

## What I built

A fairness auditing platform. It computes standard metrics —

- **Demographic parity** — does the model predict positive outcomes at equal
  rates across groups?
- **Equalized odds** — are true positive and false positive rates equal across
  groups?
- **Calibration** — are probability outputs consistent across demographics?

— and maps them directly onto EU AI Act risk categories, NIST AI RMF governance
functions and ISO/IEC 25059.

## The three hard problems

**Confounding variables.** It is not enough to detect a disparity; you have to
understand *why*. I built causal inference modules to distinguish genuine model
bias from confounding in the data, and the platform is explicit about which
claims the data can and cannot support.

**The trade-off is real.** Fairness often costs accuracy. I built interactive
Pareto frontiers so stakeholders can see exactly what they are giving up,
because that choice is a governance decision, not an engineering one.

**Bias arrives late.** It creeps in as data distributions shift. A one-off audit
misses it, so the platform tracks metrics over successive evaluation windows
and flags drift.

## What I found

Running it against a public, de-identified healthcare triage dataset surfaced
a **23% performance gap** across demographic groups. The model was
significantly less accurate for certain populations.

That number is the reason this exists. It was not subtle — a team using such a
model in production would almost certainly have noticed bad outcomes without
understanding why. Cutting a manual fairness review from days to under an hour
is the other result that matters operationally.

**Fairness is not a feature. It is a requirement.**
