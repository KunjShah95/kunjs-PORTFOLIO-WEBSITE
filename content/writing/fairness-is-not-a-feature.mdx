I started this project because I was angry.

Healthcare algorithms were making decisions about patient care — who gets
prioritised, what treatment is recommended, which patients need follow-up. And
nobody was checking whether those systems were fair.

## Why it is worse here than elsewhere

A model trained mostly on data from one demographic will perform worse for
others. That is not a bug; it is how learning from skewed data works.

Everywhere else that is a quality problem. In healthcare it is a clinical one. A
triage system that underestimates severity can delay critical care. A treatment
recommender that works better for one group produces unequal outcomes, and
nobody in the loop is in a position to notice.

And the people who could audit these models are not the people who deploy them.
The tooling gap is the real barrier — a compliance audit is expensive and slow,
so in practice it does not happen.

## The part I did not expect

I went in thinking fairness was a property to maximise. It is not. Fairness and
accuracy genuinely conflict, and any product that presents them as
independently improvable is selling something that does not exist.

The platform shows the Pareto frontier — the actual curve between accuracy and
each fairness metric — and lets a stakeholder choose a point on it. That choice
is a governance decision about whose errors are acceptable. Engineering should
make it visible, not make it.

## Why attribution comes first

The first version mapped raw metrics straight to compliance language. It
produced confident, specific, and occasionally entirely wrong regulatory
findings — because the metric was real but the cause was not ours to attribute.

A finding that says *this model is unlawful* is useless if the underlying gap
turns out to be a sampling artefact in one ward. So the pipeline computes the
metric, then tests for confounds, and only then maps to an obligation. The
extra step slowed the tool down and made it considerably more useful.

## What I found

23% performance gap across demographic groups on a public triage dataset. Not
subtle. A team using that model would have seen bad outcomes and had no way to
know why.

A manual review that took days now takes under an hour and produces a
reproducible artefact.

**Fairness is not a feature. It is a requirement.**
